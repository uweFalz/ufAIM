\chapter{Motivation}

\section{The Hidden Complexity of Railway Alignments}

At first glance, a railway alignment appears to be a simple geometric
object: a curve connecting two points in space.

However, this view is misleading.
An alignment is not merely a curve, but the result of a complex
interaction between geometry, physics, engineering constraints,
and operational requirements.

Even small changes in curvature or cant can have significant effects on:
\begin{itemize}
\item passenger comfort,
\item vehicle stability,
\item track wear,
\item and operational safety.
\end{itemize}

\section{Fragmentation of Current Practice}

In current engineering practice, alignment design is typically divided
into separate domains:
\begin{itemize}
\item geometric design (plan and profile),
\item cant design,
\item dynamic evaluation,
\item and regulatory verification.
\end{itemize}

These aspects are often treated sequentially rather than jointly.
As a consequence:
\begin{itemize}
\item design iterations are required,
\item optimal solutions are rarely achieved,
\item and knowledge remains fragmented across tools and disciplines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations of Existing Models}

Traditional alignment models are primarily geometric.
They describe the track as a sequence of elements such as:
\begin{itemize}
\item straight lines,
\item circular arcs,
\item and transition curves.
\end{itemize}

While this representation is sufficient for construction,
it is insufficient for:
\begin{itemize}
\item capturing dynamic behaviour,
\item integrating measurement data,
\item or supporting automated optimization.
\end{itemize}

In particular, curvature is often treated as a derived quantity,
although it is the fundamental driver of railway dynamics.

\section{Need for a Unified Representation}

A modern alignment model must integrate:
\begin{itemize}
\item geometry,
\item curvature evolution,
\item cant,
\item and dynamic effects.
\end{itemize}

Such a model should not treat these aspects independently,
but as components of a single coherent system.

This requires a shift from element-based descriptions to
function-based representations.

\section{Alignment as a State Trajectory}

Instead of viewing an alignment as a static geometric object,
it can be interpreted as a trajectory of a system evolving along
arc length.

In this interpretation:
\begin{itemize}
\item curvature acts as a control variable,
\item cant represents an additional state component,
\item and the resulting motion determines physical behaviour.
\end{itemize}

This perspective naturally connects alignment design with
modern concepts from:
\begin{itemize}
\item differential geometry,
\item numerical analysis,
\item and optimal control theory.
\end{itemize}

\section{Towards Alignment-Based Information Modelling}

The observations above motivate the development of a new modelling
paradigm: \emph{Alignment-Based Information Modelling} (AIM).

The central idea of AIM is to treat alignment not as a collection of
geometric primitives, but as a structured, functional object defined
by curvature and its derived quantities.

Within this framework:
\begin{itemize}
\item transitions are primary modelling elements,
\item dynamics are embedded in the representation,
\item and optimization becomes a natural design tool.
\end{itemize}

\section{Connection to Practical Engineering}

Despite its mathematical formulation, the AIM approach is not purely
theoretical.

It directly addresses practical challenges such as:
\begin{itemize}
\item handling imperfect measurement data,
\item integrating multiple coordinate systems,
\item modelling complex track configurations,
\item and supporting modern BIM workflows.
\end{itemize}

\section{Objective of this Work}

The objective of this manuscript is to provide a coherent foundation
for Alignment-Based Information Modelling.

This includes:
\begin{itemize}
\item a mathematical description of alignment structures,
\item a unified state-based representation,
\item methods for numerical reconstruction,
\item integration of engineering constraints,
\item and approaches to optimization.
\end{itemize}

\begin{summary}
Railway alignments are not simple geometric curves, but complex
systems governed by curvature, cant, and dynamic effects.

A unified, curvature-driven modelling approach is required to
capture this complexity and to enable modern, optimization-based
design methods.

This work develops the foundations of such an approach.
\end{summary}
