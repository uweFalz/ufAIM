\chapter{Curvature Transitions}

\label{chap:curvature-transitions}

% ============================================================
\section*{Motivation}
% ============================================================

The running alignment has reached the boundary $s_1$ between its straight
and transition domains. Its curvature must leave zero and reach the constant
arc curvature at $s_2$. A coordinate interpolant could connect the same end
positions while concealing how curvature and its derivatives behave at those
boundaries. The required object is therefore a controlled curvature-transition
process belonging to this identified alignment state.

\begin{principle}[Curvature-transition principle]
\label{princ:curvature-transition}
Within AIM, a transition curve is primarily a law of curvature evolution,
not a coordinate-space interpolation object.
\end{principle}

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Principle}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Curvature transition]
\label{def:curvature-transition}
A curvature transition is a curvature law:
\[
\kappa(s)
\]
connecting two curvature states:
\[
\kappa_A
\rightarrow
\kappa_B.
\]
\end{definition}

The transition determines:
\begin{itemize}
\item geometric smoothness,
\item dynamic behaviour,
\item realization stability,
\item runtime robustness,
\item and operational comfort.
\end{itemize}

\begin{principle}[Transition principle]
\label{princ:transition-principle}
Within AIM, transition geometry is governed primarily by curvature
evolution rather than by coordinate interpolation.
\end{principle}

% ============================================================
\section{Normalized Transition Space}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Normalized transition parameter]
\label{def:normalized-transition-parameter}
Transitions are commonly normalized to:
\[
w\in[0,1].
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Normalized curvature law]
\label{def:normalized-transition-law}
A normalized transition law satisfies:
\[
\hat\kappa(w)\in[0,1].
\]
\end{definition}

Normalization separates:
\begin{itemize}
\item intrinsic transition shape,
\item from physical scaling.
\end{itemize}

This enables:
\begin{itemize}
\item reusable transition families,
\item family interpolation,
\item lookup-based evaluation,
\item normalized optimization,
\item and compact sparse representation.
\end{itemize}

A physical transition is obtained by scaling normalized curvature and
normalized length to the actual curvature interval and physical
arc-length.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Families}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Transition family]
\label{def:transition-family}
A transition family is a class of normalized curvature laws sharing
common structural properties.
\end{definition}

Examples include:
\begin{itemize}
\item clothoids,
\item polynomial transitions,
\item trigonometric transitions,
\item Bloss transitions,
\item Vienna transitions,
\item lookup-based transitions,
\item and hybrid transition families.
\end{itemize}

Within AIM, transition families are interpreted as structured regions or
parameterized paths within normalized curvature space.

% ============================================================
\section{Clothoid Transition}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Clothoid]
\label{def:clothoid-transition}
The clothoid is defined by linear curvature evolution:
\[
\kappa(s)
=
a s + b.
\]
\end{definition}

In normalized form, the clothoid corresponds to:
\[
\hat\kappa(w)=w.
\]

The clothoid provides constant curvature gradient.

Historically, the clothoid became dominant because:
\begin{itemize}
\item it is simple,
\item analytically tractable,
\item compatible with classical railway practice,
\item and dynamically smoother than curvature jumps.
\end{itemize}

Within AIM, the clothoid is not treated as the universal transition
truth, but as one important member of curvature-transition space.

% ============================================================
\section{Polynomial Transitions}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Polynomial transition]
\label{def:polynomial-transition}
A polynomial transition satisfies:
\[
\kappa(s)
=
\sum_{i=0}^n a_i s^i.
\]
\end{definition}

Polynomial transitions allow:
\begin{itemize}
\item higher continuity,
\item endpoint constraints,
\item derivative constraints,
\item and optimized dynamic behaviour.
\end{itemize}

Polynomial transitions are especially useful when smoothness conditions
are imposed directly on curvature derivatives.

% ============================================================
\section{Trigonometric Transitions}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Trigonometric transition]
\label{def:trigonometric-transition}
A trigonometric transition contains sinusoidal or cosine-based curvature
components.
\end{definition}

These transitions often provide smoother higher-order behaviour than
low-degree polynomial transitions.

Trigonometric transitions are naturally connected to spectral
interpretation of curvature space.

% ============================================================
\section{Half-Wave Interpretation}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Half-wave transition]
\label{def:half-wave-transition}
A half-wave transition is a normalized curvature segment connecting:
\[
0 \rightarrow 1
\]
or
\[
1 \rightarrow 0.
\]
\end{definition}

Within AIM, many transition families are interpreted compositionally as:
\[
\text{halfWave}_1
\rightarrow
\text{core}
\rightarrow
\text{halfWave}_2.
\]

This enables:
\begin{itemize}
\item modular transition construction,
\item asymmetric transitions,
\item family interpolation,
\item and transition deformation.
\end{itemize}

The half-wave interpretation provides a common language for comparing
classical transition families and newly constructed transition laws.

\section{Berlinish Composition}
\label{sec:berlinish-composition}
% ============================================================

\emph{Berlinish} is the name used in the originating research for the
generalized transition composition
\[
T(s)
=
H_{\mathrm{in}}(s)
+
C(s)
+
H_{\mathrm{out}}(s).
\]
Here \(H_{\mathrm{in}}\) is an entering half-wave, \(C\) is an optional
clothoid section, and \(H_{\mathrm{out}}\) is an exiting half-wave. The plus
sign denotes ordered piecewise composition on consecutive subdomains, not
addition of three overlapping curvature values. Each component may have zero
length.

For a normalized transition coordinate $w\in[0,1]$, let the evidenced
partition be
\[
\lambda=(\lambda_1,\lambda_c,\lambda_2),\qquad
\lambda_i\geq0,\qquad \lambda_1+\lambda_c+\lambda_2=1.
\]
The current executable interpretation places the components on
\[
H_{\mathrm{in}}:[0,w_1],\qquad
C:[w_1,w_2],\qquad
H_{\mathrm{out}}:[w_2,1],
\]
with
\begin{equation}
w_1=\lambda_1,\qquad
w_2=\lambda_1+\lambda_c,\qquad
\lambda=(w_1,w_2-w_1,1-w_2).
\label{eq:berlinish-partition-cuts}
\end{equation}
Thus $w_1,w_2$ are cumulative normalized component-length fractions and
domain cuts in the present implementation. For a physical transition length
$L>0$, the corresponding component lengths are $L\lambda_i$. Research
evidence supports this current meaning, but only moderately supports the
claim that the historical Berlinish source used the same symbols. They are
editable partition variables today; the current bounded AXTRAN optimization
does not establish a general solve for them.

This single grammar includes important limiting cases:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(L_{H_{\mathrm{in}}}=L_{H_{\mathrm{out}}}=0\) gives a pure clothoid;
\item \(L_C=0\) gives a purely curved or smoothed half-wave composition;
\item one zero-length half-wave gives a one-sided composition;
\item unequal component lengths or different half-wave functions give
asymmetric transitions;
\item nonzero lengths for all three components give compound transitions.
\end{itemize}

Zero normalized component length removes a component from this composition.
It is not the same case as a physical transition element with total length
(L=0), for which no longitudinal domain remains. Keeping these degeneracies
separate prevents a valid pure-clothoid partition from being confused with a
zero-length physical element.

Polynomial, trigonometric, and mixed function families can supply the
half-waves. Their behaviour is compared not only through \(\kappa(s)\), but
also through
\[
\kappa'(s),\qquad \kappa''(s),
\]
because endpoint conditions and higher-order behaviour distinguish forms that
may generate visually similar centerlines. In this way, established national,
historical, or proprietary transition forms can be interpreted within one
comparison framework without losing their names or provenance.

Four levels must remain distinct. \emph{Function identity} states which
polynomial, trigonometric, or mixed law is used. \emph{Parameterization}
states how that law is scaled and partitioned. \emph{Boundary conditions}
state the curvatures, derivatives, pose conditions, and available lengths a
particular connection must satisfy. The \emph{instantiated transition
geometry} is the calculated result after those choices have been solved and
integrated. A family is therefore not an alignment element, and a plotted
curve is not the family definition.

\caption{Berlinish comparison: pure clothoid, half-wave--clothoid--half-wave composition, zero-length component cases, and optional separation of curvature and cant development.}

The lower comparison in \cref{fig:berlinish-composition} makes a further
freedom explicit. Curvature \(\kappa(s)\) describes plan-direction change per
unit intrinsic length; cant \(u(s)\) describes the transverse rail-level or
roll state under its stated convention. Speed makes their combined development
operationally consequential, but they are not the same engineering quantity.
They may use distinct function instances, domains, physical scaling,
partitions, boundary conditions, units, and provenance. Optional coupling is a
constraint between the laws, not identity of them.

The current transition registry and evaluator implement curvature only; they
do not establish a cant-law record or a validated joint curvature--cant
optimizer. Heritage Vienna examples show why cant derivatives may enter a
coupled dynamic relation, but surviving evidence does not select one
authoritative modern coupling equation or objective. Joint optimization and a
single canonical coupling therefore remain Research, not established AIM
capability.

\begin{remark}[Authority of the Berlinish term]
Berlinish is used in this thesis for an originating research contribution and
mathematical framework. Its component interpretation is implemented in the
current transition registry, but the name itself is not asserted to be an
approved Knowledge Kernel concept.
\end{remark}

\section{Curvature Anchors}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Curvature anchors]
\label{def:curvature-anchors}
Normalized transition families may define anchor values:
\[
0,
\quad
c_1,
\quad
c_2,
\quad
1.
\]
\end{definition}

Anchors describe:
\begin{itemize}
\item transition partitioning,
\item continuity conditions,
\item normalization structure,
\item and family composition.
\end{itemize}

Curvature anchors make it possible to compare transition families by
their internal curvature structure rather than by their external
position-space appearance.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Continuity}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Transition continuity]
\label{def:transition-continuity}
Transition quality depends on continuity of:
\[
\kappa(s),
\qquad
\kappa'(s),
\qquad
\kappa''(s).
\]
\end{definition}

Higher continuity improves:
\begin{itemize}
\item jerk behaviour,
\item realization smoothness,
\item runtime stability,
\item vehicle response,
\item and passenger comfort.
\end{itemize}

Continuity in position space alone is insufficient for evaluating
transition quality.

% ============================================================
\section{Sparse Transition Representation}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Sparse transition]
\label{def:sparse-transition}
A sparse transition representation stores:
\begin{itemize}
\item transition family identity,
\item normalization parameters,
\item curvature anchors,
\item scaling parameters,
\item and reconstruction rules.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Within AIM, sparse transitions are interpreted as compact curvature
operators rather than dense geometric curves.

A sparse transition therefore stores how curvature shall evolve, not a
sampled polyline approximation of the resulting geometry.

% ============================================================
\section{Lookup-Based Transitions}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Lookup-based transition]
\label{def:lookup-based-transition}
A lookup-based transition uses:
\begin{itemize}
\item sampled normalized curvature,
\item interpolation rules,
\item derivative reconstruction,
\item and runtime evaluation.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

This enables:
\begin{itemize}
\item arbitrary transition families,
\item experimentally designed transitions,
\item realization-adapted transitions,
\item vehicle-specific transitions,
\item and runtime-efficient evaluation.
\end{itemize}

Lookup-based transitions are not a fallback for missing theory. They are
a practical representation of transition laws in normalized curvature
space.

\section{Transition Derivatives}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Transition derivatives]
\label{def:transition-derivatives}
Important quantities include:
\[
\kappa'(s),
\qquad
\kappa''(s),
\qquad
\kappa'''(s).
\]
\end{definition}

These derivatives influence:
\begin{itemize}
\item jerk,
\item dynamic smoothness,
\item realization behaviour,
\item vehicle response,
\item and optimization stability.
\end{itemize}

Derivative behaviour is one of the main differences between transition
families that may otherwise appear similar in position space.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Symmetry}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Symmetric transition]
\label{def:transition-symmetry}
A normalized transition is symmetric if its curvature evolution is
symmetric with respect to the midpoint of the transition interval.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Asymmetric transition]
\label{def:transition-asymmetry}
A transition is asymmetric if this symmetry is intentionally violated.
\end{definition}

Asymmetric transitions may improve:
\begin{itemize}
\item realization quality,
\item vehicle dynamics,
\item topology fitting,
\item or geometric constraint satisfaction.
\end{itemize}

Symmetry is therefore a classification property, not a universal
requirement.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Frequency Behaviour}
% ============================================================

Transitions may be interpreted spectrally through their curvature
content.

High-frequency curvature behaviour influences:
\begin{itemize}
\item realization sensitivity,
\item machine smoothing,
\item measurement sensitivity,
\item and dynamic roughness.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}[Spectral transition principle]
\label{prop:spectral-transition-principle}
Transition quality depends not only on curvature continuity, but also on
spectral behaviour within curvature space.
\end{proposition}

This connects transition theory directly to realization-stable curvature
and curvature bandwidth.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Realization}
% ============================================================

The transition that is designed is not necessarily the transition that
is physically realized.

Realization may modify transition behaviour through:
\begin{itemize}
\item sampling,
\item machine response,
\item structural filtering,
\item ballast mechanics,
\item measurement feedback,
\item and local correction limits.
\end{itemize}

Thus, transition quality must be evaluated both in target curvature space
and in expected realized curvature space.

% ============================================================
\section{Transition Optimization}
% ============================================================

Within AIM, optimization acts primarily on:
\begin{itemize}
\item transition families,
\item normalization structure,
\item transition lengths,
\item curvature anchors,
\item derivative behaviour,
\item and curvature evolution.
\end{itemize}

Thus, optimization is interpreted primarily as optimization in
curvature-transition space rather than position space.

A realization-aware optimization may optimize not only the target
transition law \(\kappa_{\mathrm{target}}\), but also the expected
realized transition law:
\[
\kappa_{\mathrm{real}}
=
\mathcal R_{\kappa}
(
\kappa_{\mathrm{target}}
).
\]

\section{Connection to AIM}
% ============================================================

\begin{summary}
Curvature transitions form the dynamic geometric core of AIM.

Within this framework:
\begin{itemize}
\item transitions are interpreted as curvature evolution processes,
\item sparse models become transition operators,
\item normalized transition space enables reusable families,
\item half-wave decomposition enables modular transition construction,
\item lookup-based laws enable experimental and realization-aware
transition design,
\item and runtime systems reconstruct geometry dynamically from
curvature structure.
\end{itemize}

This interpretation unifies transition theory, curvature classification,
realization behaviour, runtime evaluation, and optimization within one
intrinsic curvature framework.
\end{summary}
