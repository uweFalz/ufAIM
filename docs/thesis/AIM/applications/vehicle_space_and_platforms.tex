\chapter{Vehicle Space and Platform Interaction}

\section*{Motivation}

\begin{remark}
Classical railway alignment modelling is primarily based on the
track centerline.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
In this view, the railway vehicle is treated indirectly through:
\begin{itemize}
\item clearance envelopes,
\item cant rules,
\item safety margins,
\item and operational standards.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This abstraction has proven remarkably successful and enabled the
development of reliable railway systems over many decades.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
However, the physically relevant object is not the centerline itself,
but the spatial motion of the railway vehicle along the track.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This becomes particularly visible in:
\begin{itemize}
\item tight urban rail systems,
\item platform-edge design,
\item articulated vehicles,
\item narrow tunnel geometries,
\item and highly optimized clearance situations.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This perspective is closely related to approaches that interpret
alignment geometry from the viewpoint of vehicle motion rather than
purely from the track centerline.

Such concepts have been explored in advanced railway engineering,
including Schwerpunkt-oriented alignment approaches
\cite{Hasslinger2002Schwerpunkt}.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Centerline as Reduced Representation}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
The classical alignment model represents the railway track by:
\[
\gamma(s),
\qquad
\kappa(s),
\qquad
\phi(s).
\]
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This defines:
\begin{itemize}
\item position,
\item curvature,
\item and cant,
\end{itemize}
along the alignment.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The vehicle itself is not explicitly represented.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Instead, its behaviour is incorporated indirectly through engineering
rules and geometric tolerances.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
The centerline representation is a reduced-order approximation of the
actual spatial motion of railway vehicles.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This approximation is generally sufficient for conventional railway
engineering.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Vehicle-Induced Geometry}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
A railway vehicle does not follow the centerline as a rigid point.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Its occupied space depends on:
\begin{itemize}
\item bogie geometry,
\item wheelbase,
\item car-body length,
\item articulation,
\item cant,
\item suspension,
\item and dynamic motion.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
As a consequence:
\begin{itemize}
\item vehicle ends may swing outward,
\item the center of the car body may move inward,
\item and the effective occupied space varies along the track.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\section{Platform Interaction}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
Platform-edge design is one of the most sensitive examples of
vehicle-space interaction.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The effective gap between vehicle and platform depends on:
\begin{itemize}
\item curvature,
\item cant,
\item vehicle geometry,
\item suspension state,
\item and operational conditions.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
In some urban railway systems, such as light rail and metro systems,
vehicle overhang is explicitly considered in platform-edge geometry.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This demonstrates that the practically relevant geometry is not merely
the track centerline, but the dynamically occupied vehicle space.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Standards and Conservative Approximation}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
Conventional railway standards often address vehicle motion indirectly
through conservative clearance envelopes.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
This approach increases robustness and simplifies engineering workflows.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
However, it may also lead to:
\begin{itemize}
\item increased platform gaps,
\item reduced passenger comfort,
\item larger-than-necessary clearance reserves,
\item and loss of geometric precision.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Thus, many existing systems compensate modelling limitations through
additional safety margins.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Towards Vehicle-Centric Alignment Modelling}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
Future alignment systems may extend the classical centerline model
towards vehicle-centric representations.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
In such a framework, the relevant object would no longer be only:
\[
\gamma(s),
\]
but the occupied vehicle space evolving along the alignment.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Possible extensions include:
\begin{itemize}
\item dynamic vehicle envelopes,
\item bogie-constrained motion models,
\item passenger-space optimization,
\item platform interaction models,
\item and runtime vehicle-state evaluation.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Within the AIM framework, such concepts are regarded as potential future
extensions rather than immediate replacement of existing engineering
practice.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Connection to Schwerpunkt-Trassierung}
% ============================================================

\begin{remark}
The concept of Schwerpunkt-Trassierung already shifts the interpretation
of alignment design from purely geometric construction toward the motion
of a dynamically relevant state.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Vehicle-space modelling can be interpreted as a continuation of this
idea.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Instead of optimizing only a centerline curve, one may eventually seek
to optimize the spatial behaviour of vehicles and passengers along the
track.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{Connection to AIM}
% ============================================================

\begin{summary}
Classical railway alignment modelling represents the railway track by a
centerline-based geometric abstraction.

This abstraction is highly successful, but does not explicitly represent
the spatial behaviour of railway vehicles.

Practical situations such as platform interaction reveal the limitations
of purely centerline-based modelling and motivate future vehicle-centric
extensions.

Within the AIM framework, this is regarded as an important long-term
research direction.
\end{summary}
