\chapter{Dynamic Balance}

\label{chap:dynamic-balance}

% ============================================================
\section*{Motivation}
% ============================================================

Operational railway motion is governed by the interaction between
curvature, cant, and velocity.

A railway vehicle travelling through a curve experiences lateral
acceleration generated by curvature.
Cant partially compensates this acceleration by rotating the local
vehicle reference frame.

The resulting balance between both effects is one of the central
quantities of railway operation.

Within AIM, dynamic balance is interpreted as a runtime-state relation
derived from railway and vehicle states.

\begin{principle}[Dynamic-balance principle]
\label{princ:dynamic-balance}
Dynamic balance emerges from the interaction between curvature,
cant, and operational velocity.
\end{principle}

% ============================================================
\section{Balance Relation}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Balance relation]
\label{def:balance-relation}

A dynamically balanced state approximately satisfies

\[
v^2\kappa(s)
=
g\sin\phi(s).
\]

\end{definition}

The balance relation expresses equilibrium between curvature-induced
lateral acceleration and cant-induced compensation.

A perfectly balanced state produces no residual lateral acceleration in
the local vehicle frame.

% ============================================================
\section{Effective Lateral Acceleration}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Effective lateral acceleration]
\label{def:dynamics-effective-lateral-acceleration}

The effective lateral acceleration is

\[
a_{\mathrm{eff}}
=
v^2\kappa
-
g\sin\phi .
\]

\end{definition}

The quantity \(a_{\mathrm{eff}}\) measures the residual imbalance
remaining after cant compensation.

It is one of the most important operational quantities in railway
engineering because it directly relates geometry to passenger
perception.

curvature, cant, and velocity combine into one interpretable balance
relation for runtime assessment?

Its interpretation is operational rather than decorative: balance is the
state relation produced by this coupling, and \(a_{\mathrm{eff}}\)
quantifies deviation from that relation. The engineering consequence is
that admissibility and comfort assessments must evaluate balance
trajectories, not isolated geometric parameters.

% ============================================================
\section{Cant Deficiency and Excess Cant}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Cant deficiency]
\label{def:cant-deficiency}

Cant deficiency occurs whenever

\[
a_{\mathrm{eff}}
>
0.
\]

\end{definition}

In this case the available cant is insufficient for the current
operating velocity.

\begin{definition}[Excess cant]
\label{def:excess-cant}

Excess cant occurs whenever

\[
a_{\mathrm{eff}}
<
0.
\]

\end{definition}

In this case the cant exceeds the amount required for dynamic balance.

Both situations represent operational imbalance states.

% ============================================================
\section{Balance Trajectories}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Balance trajectory]
\label{def:dynamics-balance-trajectory}

A balance trajectory is the evolution of

\[
a_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
\]

along the alignment.

\end{definition}

Balance trajectories are generated jointly by

\begin{itemize}
\item curvature evolution,
\item cant evolution,
\item and velocity evolution.
\end{itemize}

Operational smoothness depends not only on the magnitude of imbalance
but also on how imbalance changes along the route.

% ============================================================
\section{Operational Interpretation}
% ============================================================

Dynamic balance forms the connection between

\begin{itemize}
\item railway geometry,
\item railway state,
\item vehicle-space interpretation,
\item and passenger comfort.
\end{itemize}

It therefore occupies a central position between the geometric and
dynamic layers of AIM.

Balance is not a geometric property.

Balance is an operational state generated from geometry.

% ============================================================
\section{Canonical Interpretation}
% ============================================================

\begin{proposition}

Within AIM, dynamic balance is interpreted as a runtime-derived
operational state.

\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}

Effective lateral acceleration measures the degree of operational
imbalance.

\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}

Cant deficiency and excess cant are complementary imbalance states.

\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}

Balance trajectories describe the evolution of operational balance
along the alignment.

\end{proposition}

% ============================================================
\section{Connection to AIM}
% ============================================================

\begin{summary}

Dynamic balance provides the fundamental coupling between curvature,
cant, and operational velocity.

Within AIM,

\begin{itemize}
\item balance is interpreted as an operational state,
\item effective acceleration quantifies imbalance,
\item cant deficiency and excess cant describe deviation from
equilibrium,
\item and balance trajectories characterize operational behaviour along
the alignment.
\end{itemize}

This chapter establishes the balance concepts required for
Schwerpunkt-Trassierung, realization-aware dynamics, admissibility
evaluation, and optimization.

\end{summary}
